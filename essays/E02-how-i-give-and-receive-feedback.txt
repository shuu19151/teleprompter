HOW I GIVE AND RECEIVE FEEDBACK
AT WORK

When people ask me about the hardest
part of office life, I usually say,
"It's not the deadlines.

It's talking about the work honestly."

I work in an office in the
city, and every week someone needs to
hear what went well and what still
needs work.

For a long time, I was afraid
of those talks.

I didn't want to hurt anyone, and
I didn't want to feel small myself.

Over the years, I've learned that feedback
is a skill, just like writing
a report.

Today I'd like to share a few
habits that have helped me a lot.

The first habit is asking for feedback
ahead of time, not only after something
goes wrong.

Every month, I send a short message
to my manager and one teammate.

I ask two simple questions: "What should
I keep doing?" and "What should
I change?"

I used to wait until the yearly
review, and then I felt shocked by
comments I had never heard before.

Now I hear small notes early, when
they are still easy to fix.

After a presentation, I also ask one
person in the room, "Was anything unclear?"

It takes two minutes, but it saves
me from repeating the same mistakes.

The second habit is listening without
defending myself.

At first, when someone said my report
was too long, I immediately explained why
every page was necessary.

I talked so much that I didn't
really hear them.

One Tuesday afternoon, my manager sat down
with me after a client call.

She said, "The client got lost in
the details.

Next time, start with the result."

I wanted to say that the client
had asked for details.

Instead, I wrote her words down and
said, "Thank you.

I'll try that."

Since then, I wait five seconds before
I reply.

Those five seconds help me stay open.

In the moment, my job is
to understand.

The third habit is being specific when
I give feedback to others.

Vague comments like "This isn't good" or
"Great job" don't help anyone.

Now I try to name one thing
that worked and one thing to improve.

Last week, a new colleague sent me
a draft email to a client.

The tone was friendly, but the request
was buried at the end.

I told her, "The greeting is warm,
and that's great.

Could we put the deadline in the
first two lines?"

She smiled and changed it in
a minute.

Because I was clear, she didn't
feel attacked.

She just knew what to do next.

Another thing that makes a big difference
is choosing the right time and place.

I never give serious feedback in a
busy group chat or in front of
the whole team.

If I need to talk about a
missed deadline, I book a short call
or walk over to their desk when
they look free.

I also check my own mood.

If I'm tired or annoyed, I wait
until the next morning.

A calm ten-minute talk is better than
a fast message that the other person
reads three times and worries about
all evening.

Of course, there are still difficult days.

A few months ago, I received written
comments on a project I had spent
two weeks on.

The notes were short and quite hard:
"This is not what we asked for.

Please redo it."

I felt heat in my face.

I wanted to reply immediately and list
all the hours I had spent.

Instead, I stepped away, made a tea,
and came back twenty minutes later.

In a meeting I said, "I want
to get this right.

Can we look at the original
request together?"

We found that I had missed one
line in the brief.

It was my mistake.

I also asked myself, "What can I
control right now?"

I could not change the comments, but
I could make a new plan and
meet the new deadline.

Finally, I try to keep feedback in
its place, so it doesn't fill my
whole evening.

After I leave the office, I don't
reread critical emails on the sofa.

I write one action for the next
day, then I close the laptop.

At first, it was difficult to switch
off, especially after a tough review.

Now I understand that rest helps me
use the feedback well.

When I sleep enough, I can look
at comments with a clearer head.

In conclusion, giving and receiving feedback at
work doesn't require a special talent.

It just needs a few simple habits:
ask ahead of time, listen before you
defend, be specific, choose a good moment,
and protect your evenings.

I'm still learning, and some conversations still
make my heart beat faster.

But these habits have already made my
work life more honest and less scary,
and I would encourage anyone to
try them.

Thank you for listening.
