ONE MONTH WITHOUT SMOKING

When people ask how much I smoke,
I usually say, “About one cigarette
a day.”

Then I think about how many parts
of my day are connected to that
one cigarette.

I've gone a week without smoking, and
I'm giving myself a month to see
what changes.

Today I'd like to share why I
started and what I want to pay
attention to.

The first thing that got me thinking
was a book a friend gave me.

The idea behind it is simple: try
one challenge every month and build a
healthier life over a year.

A month felt like a period I
could picture in my own life.

I could start with an ordinary week
and come back to the question a
few weeks later.

So I chose to spend the month
without cigarettes.

At the end, I want to remember
what the month was like while it
was happening.

The second thing I've noticed is how
closely smoking fits into my daily routine.

I often had a cigarette around lunchtime,
after waking up from a nap.

Other times I'd have that day's cigarette
in the evening, just before bed.

And when I sit down with an
espresso, the thought of smoking comes
up easily.

I can picture the small cup in
front of me and the familiar pause
between sips.

The coffee is still there, and I
can take a short break and finish
it at my own pace.

That's one of the moments I want
to watch over the next three weeks.

At work, part of the habit
is social.

Some coworkers smoke, and when we step
away from our desks, they often offer
me a cigarette.

It's a casual offer, the kind you
can accept before you give it
much thought.

If someone offers me one again, I
can keep my answer simple: “Thanks,
I'll pass.”

I can still catch up with a
coworker and hear how their day
is going.

I'd like to see how that moment
feels after I've turned down a
few offers.

Maybe saying it will feel as ordinary
as accepting once did.

Another thing I want to understand is
what happens when I'm alone at home.

Sometimes I have nothing planned, and I
get bored.

That's when a cigarette can seem like
a way to give the next few
minutes something to do.

Picture an evening when I'm home by
myself and there's still time before bed.

In that moment, I can ask myself,
“What do I actually want to do
with these ten minutes?”

The answer might be as simple as
opening my book or staying where I
am for a while.

During this first week, I simply
stopped smoking.

I didn't set up a new routine,
though I can read a few pages
when I have time to spare.

When I reach the end of the
month, I'd like to look back on
a few specific moments.

Which felt harder, sitting with an espresso
or turning down an offer from
a coworker?

Did I think about cigarettes at the
same times in the fourth week as
I did in the first?

I want to see whether those thoughts
took up less space as the days
went on.

I can check in with myself on
a quiet evening and think through what
happened that day.

A few examples will help me remember
how I got through the month.

I also want to learn from the
people around me.

My friend's gift showed me how a
small gesture can give someone an idea
to try.

I'm curious about how other people deal
with habits that are tied to a
time or a place.

If it comes up in conversation, I
might ask a coworker what helped them
stick with a change.

They might tell me about something I
can try, or describe a moment that
sounds familiar.

I'd like to hear what their first
week felt like and what they remember
about it now.

In conclusion, my first week has given
me a few things to pay attention
to: my usual breaks, offers from coworkers,
and quiet time at home.

I'm still learning how I respond in
each of those situations.

When the month ends, I hope I
can tell the story with a few
clear examples.

I want to remember the espresso I
finished, the conversations I kept having, and
what I did with my time when
I was alone.

I'd also like to know what I
learned from other people, starting with the
friend who gave me the book.

For now, I'm one week in, and
I'll see what the next espresso break
feels like.

Thank you for listening.
